What to write down for your family in case something happens to you (UK)

By Hope, who works in probate in the UK · Updated 11 November 2026

Most families don't struggle with grief alone. They struggle with a treasure hunt: which bank, which pension, where the Will is, whether there was a password to the phone. Writing a few things down now is one of the kindest things you can do.

Start with the essentials

  • Your full name, date of birth, National Insurance number and NHS number. The registrar, banks and the council will ask for these first.
  • Who your executors are, and how to contact them.
  • Where your original Will is kept, and who prepared it. Probate generally needs the original, signed Will, not a copy.
  • Whether you have Lasting Powers of Attorney, who your attorneys are, and where the documents are.

Money and property

  • Every bank and building society account, even small or dormant ones. The last four digits are enough to identify them.
  • Pensions, with the provider and policy or member number. If you've lost track of an old pension, the free Pension Tracing Service can help.
  • Savings, ISAs, investments and NS&I Premium Bonds (note your holder number).
  • Your home, and how it's owned. If it's jointly owned as joint tenants it usually passes straight to the other owner; as tenants in common, your share passes under your Will.
  • Insurance and life cover, including any policy written in trust.
  • Loans, credit cards and anything else you owe.

Everyday life

  • Bills and subscriptions that would need cancelling. The government's Tell Us Once service notifies many government departments, but not banks, energy companies or subscriptions.
  • Your digital life: where your passwords are kept (never the passwords themselves), your email and social media, and whether you've set up legacy contacts with Apple, Google or Facebook.
  • Who should care for your pets or horses, and what they need day to day.
  • Your funeral wishes, and any pre-paid funeral plan.

Keep it safe, and tell someone

  • Keep everything in one place, ideally with your Will, and tell your executor where it is.
  • Review it once a year, or after a big change such as a move, marriage, new account or new pet.

Questions

Is a list like this legally binding?

No. It's a way of organising information and wishes. To make your wishes legally binding you need a valid Will, and Lasting Powers of Attorney for decisions while you're alive.

Should I write down my passwords?

No. Write down where they are kept, for example in a password manager, so the right person can find them.

How often should I update it?

Once a year, and whenever something significant changes.
