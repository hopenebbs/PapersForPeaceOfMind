A fire and emergency plan for your livery yard (UK)

By Hope, who works in probate in the UK · Updated 11 November 2026

When something goes wrong on a yard, minutes matter. A simple plan that everyone has seen makes the difference between panic and a calm, quick response.

Who and where

  • The yard address and postcode, plus a what3words reference or clear directions to the gate. Rural yards can be hard to find at night.
  • Yard owner, manager, yard vet and out-of-hours vet.
  • An emergency contact for every livery, and whether they authorise vet treatment if they can't be reached.

Fire

  • An assembly point for people and a safe holding area for horses.
  • Where the fire extinguishers, water supply, stopcock and electricity board are.
  • Who calls 999, and who meets the fire service at the gate.
  • A headcollar and lead rope outside every stable, so anyone can lead a horse out quickly.
  • Hay stored away from stables, escape routes kept clear, and the plan shown to every livery.

Illness and new arrivals

  • Many yards isolate new arrivals for two to three weeks and ask the vet about a strangles blood test before they mix.
  • A log of any isolation and illness on the yard, and who was told.

Everyday records that help in an emergency

  • A liveries register and a horse information sheet for every horse.
  • A signed livery agreement for every horse, setting out who is responsible for what.

Questions

Does a yard need a written fire plan?

It's strongly recommended. A plan everyone has seen saves vital time. A professional fire risk assessment is also worth having.

Why hang a headcollar outside each stable?

So anyone, even someone who doesn't know the horse, can lead it out quickly in an emergency.

How long should new horses be isolated?

Many yards isolate new arrivals for two to three weeks. Ask your vet about testing.
